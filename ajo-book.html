Ajo Book

A savings group ledger designed to beat the notebook, not other apps

Summary

An ajo is a rotating savings group, common across West Africa. Members pay in a fixed amount each round, and one member takes the whole pot in turn. One person, the holder, keeps the record, usually in a notebook and a WhatsApp list of ticks. I designed Ajo Book to make her job easier without changing how the group works. One insight shaped everything: the competitor isn't another app. It's her notebook.

Problem Definition

The problem: one person carries the whole group's trust in a notebook

The holder marks every payment by hand, posts the list on WhatsApp, calls late payers one by one, and settles arguments about who paid and whose turn it is. When the notebook is wrong, she's the one people blame.

[Illustration: Notebook and WhatsApp list]

Illustration comparing the physical handwritten ruled ledger notebook with the messy WhatsApp checkmark status broadcast.

Field Research

Research: the notebook works, and that's the problem

I interviewed 12 people in person, holders and members of ajo groups, and watched how holders keep their records. Holders didn't want their notebooks photographed, so I took notes on what I saw instead. That alone said a lot about how private and personal this record is.

What I found:

  1. The notebook is fast, familiar and trusted. Any app has to be quicker than drawing a tick, or it loses.
  2. Members want to see the record themselves. The holder gets called constantly by people asking if their payment was recorded.
  3. Sharing updates again and again wears holders out. Re-posting the list after every payment is a hidden job.
  4. When a member goes silent, there's no backup. Holders have no way to reach someone close to a member who stops answering.
  5. Swapped turns get forgotten. When two members swap, it's easy to lose track of who's next.

"If the group can have a link that's only for the members, instead of calling all the time, it will be more transparent."

— Ajo member

"Having to keep sharing will be stressful."

— Ajo holder

"We wish the app could have a guarantor's phone number, so we can reach close family when we can't reach a member."

— Ajo holder

The insight: most savings apps ask people to move their money into the app. Holders didn't want that. They wanted a better notebook that the whole group could see.

Persona & Context

Who I designed for

Mama Bisi, 48, market trader and ajo holder. Busy and respected, not very confident with technology, and her eyesight isn't what it used to be. She uses her Android phone mostly for WhatsApp and calls.

  • She needs to know instantly who has paid, and to pay the right person on time.
  • She worries about being accused of cheating or losing someone's money.
  • She won't learn a complicated app or hand the group's money to a company.

Product Principles & Iterations

Key decisions

Design principles: faster than a pen, kinder than a notebook

  1. One tap to mark someone paid. If it's slower than a tick, it fails.
  2. Easy to read in sunlight, on a cheap phone. At least 18px text and 56px buttons.
  3. Words beside every icon. Colour is never the only signal.
  4. Undo instead of "Are you sure?" Mistakes should be easy to fix, not scary to make.
  5. Fit the habits she already has. Share to WhatsApp, and call or text from the app.

What I changed, and why

Before After Why
Drag and drop to reorder turns A simple Swap button Dragging is hard on a small screen and easy to get wrong
"Are you sure?" pop-ups Undo Pop-ups slow her down; undo makes mistakes safe
WhatsApp-only reminders Call, WhatsApp or SMS for each member Not every member uses WhatsApp
Pay-out button always ready Faded until everyone has paid, with "2 people still owe" Stops her paying out too early by mistake
Different reminder options in different places The same options everywhere She wondered where options had gone

Key features

  • Mark paid in one tap, with paid and owes shown in words, not just colour
  • A members-only link, so members can check their own status, and the holder stops getting calls and re-sharing the list. This came straight from what members asked for.
  • Reminders by call, WhatsApp or SMS, depending on each member
  • Share the paid list to the WhatsApp group when she wants to
  • Swap turns and see the new order instantly
  • Undo any mistake in one tap
  • Try it with example groups before adding real names
[Image: Key screens]

Final responsive mobile web app screens: Welcome onboarding, ledger home, members view-only link, turn order matrix, and WhatsApp list format.

Market Ledger: a design system with roots

I based the look on the markets where these groups live: adire indigo (#23356B, from the traditional Yoruba dyed cloth) for trust and actions, marigold (#E8A33D) for highlights, and a warm paper background (#FAF6EE) that echoes the notebook. For body text, I chose Atkinson Hyperlegible, a typeface designed for people with low vision, and Fraunces for headings. It feels familiar, not like a bank, which matters for someone who doesn't trust financial apps.

[Image: Design system]

Market Ledger design tokens: Adire Indigo (#23356B), Marigold (#E8A33D), Warm Paper (#FAF6EE), Atkinson Hyperlegible type scale, and ledger row components.

Building with AI, directed by design

I used Bolt, an AI app builder, to turn my designs into a working app quickly, so I could test with a real holder early. Then I spent about a month improving it based on feedback. The AI wrote the code; I made the decisions. I wrote each change as a clear instruction, checked every result against my principles, and kept a log of what changed and why.

Next:

Retrospective

What I learned

Never assume while building a product. The only way to understand a problem is to talk to the people you're solving it for. My 12 interviews changed what I built. The members' link, for example, came straight from members asking for it.

Building is only half the work. After you build, put it in front of real users. Watching a holder use Ajo Book showed me problems I would never have found on my own, and drove a month of improvements.

Sometimes the best design fits into a habit instead of replacing it. I didn't need to move the group's money into an app. I needed to make the holder's job easier, and let the whole group see the record.
