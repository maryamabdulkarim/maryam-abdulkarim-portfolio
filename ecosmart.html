Summary

In Nigeria, recyclable waste is often dumped or burned. Households don't know it's worth money, and recyclers struggle to find enough material. EcoSmart AI lets people take a photo of an item, find out what it is and what it's worth, and connect with a verified recycler nearby. The hardest design problem wasn't the AI. It was getting two groups who didn't trust each other to do a deal.

Problem Space

The problem: two groups needed each other, but couldn't find or trust each other

Households saw waste as something to throw away. Recyclers spent time and money chasing material through middlemen. Both sides would have benefited from dealing directly, but there was no easy way to find each other, and no reason to trust each other.

Research & Discovery

Research: trust, not willingness, was the real barrier

I surveyed 157 households, individuals, and small and medium businesses, and spoke with 57 recyclers working with metal, plastic and paper.

01

1. People didn't know their waste had value.

Households weren't against recycling. They didn't know what was recyclable, what it was worth, or where to take it.

So the app shows what an item is and what it's worth first, before asking for anything.

02

2. Recyclers couldn't find reliable supply.

One recycler told us only about 30% of his material came directly from the people who produce it. The rest came through middlemen who took a cut.

So the app had to bring recyclers a steady supply, not just help households.

03

3. Trust was the biggest barrier.

One recycler had lost millions of naira to a fraudulent deal. Households worried about being underpaid.

So every step of a deal had to be visible, fair and traceable.

That last insight became the product's one job: make every step of a waste deal visible, fair and traceable.

Key Decisions

Key decisions

The AI admits when it might be wrong.

An AI that guesses confidently and gets it wrong loses trust fast, especially when money is involved. So the result screen shows:

  • what the item is, and whether it's recyclable
  • its value as a range, like ₦50 – ₦150, because the final amount depends on the weight
  • an "AI verified" or "Self-reported" badge, so people know where the answer came from
  • a "This doesn't look right?" link, so people can correct it
  • a clear "Not recyclable" result for photos that aren't waste, instead of a guess

The final price is fair, and either side can challenge it.

An estimate only builds trust if the final payment is fair too. In the Fair Final Amount flow:

  1. The user sees an estimated range when they scan.
  2. After the handover, the recycler enters the actual weight.
  3. The app calculates the amount at the recycler's published rate.
  4. The user accepts it or flags it as unfair.
  5. If nobody acts, it confirms itself after 24 hours.

Nobody has to take the other's word for it.

Addresses stay private until a recycler commits.

Sharing a home address with a stranger is a real safety risk. Pickup requests show only the user's area until a recycler accepts. Only then is the exact location shared, and only with that recycler.

Trade-offs I made

I chose Over Because

A design system built for low-cost phones

I designed for the phones and networks people actually have: 360px-wide screens first, 48px touch targets, light pages, and words beside every icon. The system includes 41 colour tokens in light and dark, 11 text styles (Manrope and Inter), a 4px spacing scale and 12 components, such as the AI result card, recycler card and wallet card.

An accessibility check changed the palette. White text on our brand green (#599432) only reached a contrast of 3.68:1, below the 4.5:1 minimum. I kept the green for the logo and icons, and added a deeper green (#3F7A22) for buttons and links, which passes at 5.2:1. Every status also has a word, not just a colour.

Usability Testing

Testing: the AI earned trust by admitting when something was wrong

We shared the MVP with 50+ testers from our community and asked them to try it with their own waste.

What worked: sign-up and scanning were smooth. The AI identified materials like PET bottles, aluminium and cans, and users could confirm or change the result.

"I uploaded an incorrect image first, and the platform correctly flagged it."

— MVP tester

This was the result I cared about most: the honesty I designed into the AI worked for a real user.

What testers struggled with

What testers said What we learned
"Search Nearby Recycler" found no recyclers A marketplace is only as good as its supply. Signing up recyclers matters as much as the app.
They wanted to search by area, like Kubwa (a neighbourhood in Abuja) People think in neighbourhoods, not map pins.
The estimate didn't show how it was worked out People trust a number more when they can see the sum (₦ per kg × kg).
The plastic bottle instructions contradicted each other Small mistakes weaken trust in everything else.
Some items, like old phones, were missing The list of materials has to grow with what people actually scan.

A request I turned into a choice: testers wanted automatic location. Instead of switching it on for everyone, I made it optional. People can turn on their location, or set it manually, at any time. Live location could put people at risk if it were misused, so each person decides for themselves.

What I changed: in Version 1, users contacted recyclers by WhatsApp or phone. Once a conversation left the app, we couldn't see what had been agreed, which was exactly the fraud risk our research warned about. In Version 2, all contact happens through in-app chat and calls.

Retrospective

What I learned

In a marketplace, the design problem is trust. I started by focusing on scan accuracy, but research showed that a perfect scan means nothing if people don't trust the deal.

Honest AI beats impressive AI. Testers trusted the app most when it said something wasn't right.

Supply shapes the experience. The best search screen shows nothing if there are no recyclers nearby. Next time, I'd sign up recyclers alongside user testing, not after it.
