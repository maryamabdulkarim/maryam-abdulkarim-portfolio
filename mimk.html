Summary

Booking a short stay in Nigeria often means trusting a stranger with a bank transfer, then discovering a caution fee, a diesel charge or a flat that doesn’t match its photos. Mimk lists verified hotels, apartments and shortlets. I redesigned it around three promises: every listing is inspected in person, every price includes all fees, and the host is only paid after the guest checks in. I designed the guest, host and admin sides, and although the brief asked for desktop, I designed every flow on mobile too.

Problem

A trust problem before it’s a search problem

I started with desk research, about 20 sources on fraud, fees, payments and power. Four facts shaped everything that followed.

Airbnb listings in Nigeria grew 47% in 2025 while revenue per listing fell. Guests have too many unverified choices, and 75% of Nigerian web traffic is on phones.

Problem statement: travellers in Nigeria need a way to book stays they can trust, at a price they know up front.

Research

Testing six assumptions

I turned the desk research into six guest hypotheses, then wrote a 22-question guest survey and a 10-question host survey, each question tied to a hypothesis or a business rule. The project owner asked for synthetic data for this concept, so I generated 8 simulated guests and 2 simulated hosts from the personas and desk research. They show what the personas suggest, not what the market measures.

Hypothesis Result (synthetic, 8 guests) Verdict
Listings are often misleading 4 of 8 had a mismatch or a scam attempt Supported
Hidden fees appear late 4 of 8 had unexpected costs Supported
Power drives the choice 5 of 8 put 24-hour power in their top 3 Supported
Paying a stranger feels risky Only 3 of 8 would pay more for protection Partly: build protection into the price
Verification builds trust Inspection tied with real reviews, 4 each Partly: show both together
Finding the place is hard 2 of 8 rated arrival 1–2 out of 5 Not supported: lower priority

“The photos showed a big living room; it was half the size, and they added ₦15,000 a day for diesel when we arrived.”

Synthetic respondent G-03 · family planner, Abuja

The finding that changed who Mimk is for

The counter-voices mattered most. A happy Airbnb user and a traveller whose company books their hotels didn’t need Mimk at all. Mimk’s strongest audience is people booking through Instagram or WhatsApp agents, and diaspora travellers booking from abroad. With real users, this is the first finding I’d check.

Key Decisions

Key decisions

Six principles decided every screen

  1. Show the full price. Fees are included from the first search, never added at checkout.
  2. Explain the trust. Say what “Verified” checked, when, and when the host gets paid.
  3. Hold the money. Mimk pays the host 24 hours after check-in, not before.
  4. WhatsApp first. Codes, receipts and door codes arrive where guests already are.
  5. Design every state. Pending, failed, empty, cancelled and disputed, not just the happy path.
  6. One consistent story. The same people, prices and dates across guest, host and admin.

Trade-offs I made

I chose Over Because
Bank transfer as the default Card first Transfer is how most Nigerians pay online. The risk was paying a stranger, so Mimk sits in the middle
Phone number and a WhatsApp code Email and password Five fields became three short steps, on the app guests already use
Protection built into the 5% fee Selling it as an extra Most respondents wouldn’t pay more for protection, but all of them wanted it
Refunds only for broken listed promises Refunding any complaint Hosts need rules they can trust too, and guests still get a clear answer
Design Execution

One clear action per screen

In review, some screens had four buttons competing for attention. I wrote button rules into the product brief, then rebuilt every page around them:

  • Solid blue for the one thing the person came to do. At most one per screen.
  • Outlined for a real alternative beside it, like Message next to Call.
  • Text links for actions repeated on every row, like “Book again”.
  • Red outline for anything that cancels, never solid.

On Past stays, the one review with a deadline moved out of the list into its own card, with the page’s only solid button.

Two-sided Marketplace

Hosts and admins keep the promise real

A booking platform is only as trustworthy as the people behind it. Hosts see the one request that needs them today, their earnings, and exactly when they’ll be paid. They pay 10% commission, less than Airbnb’s 15.5% host fee. Admins approve inspected listings and settle disputes.

Refunds follow listed promises

The first dispute I designed was about hot water, which the listing never promised. I changed it. A refund should only follow a broken promise the listing made: power, water, security or accuracy. So the case became “backup power failed”, and the policy suggests refunding one night if the problem is fixed within 24 hours. The admin sees the split before confirming: ₦18,667 back to the guest, and the host still receives ₦33,600.

Edge Cases

Leaving can’t cost a guest a stay

Settings used to appear in two places, the sidebar and Profile. I removed the duplicate, so Profile is the one home for personal info, payments, preferences and help. Preferences let guests choose a language and see prices in another currency, shown as an estimate, because they always pay in naira.

Blocked, not just warned

My first version let a guest delete their account with a warning that their upcoming stay would be cancelled. That put a paid booking, the host’s income and the refund at risk in one tap. Now, if a stay is coming up, deletion waits: the dialog shows the booking and the date they can delete, with “View upcoming booking” as the main action and no red button. Guests with nothing booked get a normal red confirmation.

Retrospective

What I learned

Trust lives in the details. A date, an amount and a name do more than a badge or a slogan. “Inspected on 4 Nov by Tobi A.” says more than “Verified”.

Rules before screens. I wrote down fees, refunds and payouts before drawing them, so the guest, host and admin sides all tell the same story with the same numbers.

Recommend, don’t just deliver. The brief asked for desktop. Explaining why mobile mattered, with one number, turned a request into a better product.
